The Page Before the Page

Strategy

When a landing page underperforms, the instinct is to blame the page: the headline, the button colour, the offer. But very often the real problem happened one step earlier — on the page before the page.

Every page has a previous page

Nobody arrives at your contact form from nowhere. They came from a Google result, a menu click, a footer link, a blog post. The state of mind they carry from that previous page decides how they read the current one. If the journey to your money page is confusing, the money page starts with a confused reader — and confused readers do not convert.

Navigation is a promise

Every item in your menu makes a promise about what the visitor will find. "Services" promises a clear overview. "Pricing" promises numbers. When the click leads somewhere different — a vague overview, a contact form, an unrelated page — the visitor feels the broken promise and leaves. Audit your navigation by clicking every item and asking one question: does this page deliver exactly what the label promised, in the first five seconds?

Design the journey backwards

The most reliable way to fix a funnel is to work backwards from the goal:

  • What is the conversion page? Usually contact, checkout, or a booking form.
  • What page convinces someone to go there? The service page, the case study, the pricing page.
  • What page helps someone find that page? Category pages, guides, the homepage.

Each step should answer exactly one question and point to exactly one next step. When every page tries to do everything, no page does anything.

Small structures, big results

You rarely need a redesign to fix a journey. You need breadcrumbs that reflect the real hierarchy, related links at the bottom of articles that point to services (not other articles), and a homepage that routes visitors by intent — "I know what I want" versus "I need to understand my options."
